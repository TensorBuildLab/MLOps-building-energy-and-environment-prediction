# Conclusions {#sec:conclusions}

This study divided the work of building data-driven energy models between an agent, which supplies building-specific judgment through declarations of physical quantities, and a domain package, which applies the same checks to every model, from physical plausibility to gated promotion, monitoring and retraining. A pre-specified evaluation on public data of 200 BDG2 buildings and 72 room and system tasks answers the research questions as follows.

1. RQ1: Day ahead, routinely configured models beat references that include TOWT significantly in 66% of the buildings (median skill 0.16) and in 62.5% of all tasks; elsewhere the framework kept the simple rule.
2. RQ2: As M&V baselines over a reporting year, the models matched TOWT but did not beat it (median absolute annual savings error 5.3% against 5.2%).
3. RQ3: On the same rows, shuffled cross-validation understated the error of forward chaining in all 62 tasks, by a median of 41%, and changed verdicts: in M&V it accepted 32 baselines that failed in the reporting year, against 13 for a time-ordered split.
4. RQ4: Over a year in 50 buildings, monthly retraining gave the lowest error, and drift-triggered retraining at the default tolerance was 4.8% worse in the median building. Of the models the gates promoted, 86% realized a skill of at least 0.05 over the next three months, but about half of those they refused would also have reached it.
5. RQ5: The checks detected six of eight planted fault types in every seed and found a site of daily-total meters in cleaned BDG2 data, but missed months of zero readings. Their default handling matched a competent hand-written script in mean absolute error and beat a quick script by 1.9 ppm, at a cost of about 1 ppm in RMSE on clean data.

For practice: estimate a building model's error in time order, because that estimate decides M&V verdicts; compare every model with the strongest simple rule, including TOWT, and keep the rule where the model does not beat it; retrain on a schedule and monitor the baseline alongside the model; and check meters against what they measure, not only against their own history. The framework makes these the default for whoever builds the model, a design hypothesis that this study did not test with independent users. Future work should test it in a multi-run benchmark with primed and unprimed agents, add checks for dead meters and across related sensors, make prediction intervals adaptive, take declarations from Brick models, and deploy the framework in operating buildings.
