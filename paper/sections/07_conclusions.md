# Conclusions {#sec:conclusions}

LLM agents can now write the code for a building prediction model, but whether the result can be relied on in operation depends on what surrounds that code. This study divided the work: the agent supplies the judgment that messy, building-specific data demand, and a domain package applies the same checks to every model, from declarations checked against physics to gated promotion, monitoring and retraining. The framework was evaluated on three public datasets of real buildings and in a pilot with eight independent agent runs. The answers to the research questions are as follows.

1. RQ1: One workflow produced an evaluated and gated result for all 92 tasks, covering energy, thermal load, temperature, CO~2~, humidity, PM2.5, people counts and occupancy. Ordinary models beat the strongest baseline without machine learning significantly in 52% of the tasks, and 45% failed the promotion gates; for a deployable building model, the strongest simple rule is the bar to clear.
2. RQ2: On the same data, the random split of the preliminary study understated the RMSE by a median of 27% (range 2–59%) against blocked cross-validation on every one of 22 tasks, whereas feature selection on all rows added no measurable optimism.
3. RQ3: The checks detected six of eight planted fault types in every seed, spikes in nine seeds of ten and a stuck meter at its idle floor in five; these rates are partly in-sample, because detectors were revised on the same dataset. On the one CO~2~ task with planted faults, accuracy with the checks matched the clean data, whereas a quick script was 1.6–1.9 ppm worse in MAE, with or without the same unit fix, and three to five times less stable in RMSE between seeds; the mean RMSE did not differ.
4. RQ4: Monitoring with gated retraining reduced the mean monthly RMSE of a static model by 10–33%. For district heating it only matched persistence on the mean and lost to it in five of eight months, and simple monthly retraining performed better on both tasks. Retraining should therefore be scheduled, and persistence should be monitored alongside the model.
5. RQ5: In a pilot with one run per cell, capable agents told to be rigorous built leak-free models of similar accuracy with or without the framework, with no consistent difference in either direction, and found as many planted faults, at a higher cost with the framework. All four agents that worked on the room with the dead chilled-water meter found it, whereas the case-study agent, relying on the checks, had missed it.

The measured value of the framework therefore does not lie in out-modeling a careful agent. It lies in making the time-ordered protocol and the strongest baseline the default, in producing comparable and re-executable records, and in supporting the lifecycle after the first model. Agents that used the framework were also effective testers of it. Future work should evaluate the framework in a multi-run benchmark with unprimed and weaker agents, add checks across related sensors, make prediction intervals depend on the time of day and recalibrate them in operation, take declarations from Brick models, and deploy the framework in an operating building.
