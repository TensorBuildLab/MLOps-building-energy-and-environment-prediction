# Discussion {#sec:discussion}

## When machine learning is worth deploying for building energy

Day ahead, routinely configured models beat references that include TOWT and the value a week earlier in two-thirds of the 200 buildings, with a median skill of 0.16. The improvement is useful but not universal: across all tasks the framework kept the simple rule in 87 of 272, which for an operator of many buildings saves maintenance and keeps the fallback obvious. The exploratory peak analysis suggests that where the models win, they also win on the size of each day's peak, which matters for demand response.

For annual savings the picture is different. As M&V baselines, the machine-learning candidates matched TOWT but did not beat it, and the framework itself chose TOWT or a profile in two-thirds of the buildings. Over a year, the weekly schedule and the temperature response carry most of what a model can know about consumption without information on operation, and TOWT captures both with few parameters. This supports keeping TOWT as the M&V reference [@granderson2016accuracy] and spending modeling effort where the time scale is short and recent history is informative. The hourly Guideline 14 limits, in turn, discriminated little between forecasts and their references (159 models and 150 references met them), so they mostly separate predictable meters from difficult ones. As a gate they are more useful for M&V baselines, where an error estimate decides whether a baseline is accepted.

## Evaluation protocol and the decisions it drives

A random split understated the error of every model, by a median of 41% against forward chaining on the same rows, and it changed decisions: shuffled cross-validation accepted 32 M&V baselines that then missed the hourly limits in the reporting year, and placed 8 more day-ahead models within them than forward chaining confirmed. That random splits mislead on dependent data is known [@roberts2017crossvalidation; @bergmeir2012; @kapoor2023]; what these results add is the size of the effect on building operational data and its consequence for M&V, where a baseline accepted on an optimistic error estimate turns model error into reported savings. Even a time-ordered split within the baseline year understated the reporting-year error and accepted 13 baselines that then failed, so M&V error estimates should come from time-ordered validation and be read as lower bounds. For model predictive control and demand response, an error understated by 40% would make constraint margins and flexibility offers too tight; the framework offers no random split, which is the simplest way to remove that risk.

## Lessons for the model lifecycle

Monthly retraining was the best policy, and drift-triggered retraining approached it only with a tolerance well below the default. This agrees with Li et al. [@li2023large], who found at large scale that building energy models updated with new data outperform static ones. Drift detectors have been compared across HVAC devices [@derzsi2025performance]; here, a trigger based on the ratio of recent to test-time error depended on a test-time error measured on a short window, so slow changes rarely crossed a fixed tolerance. Where a monthly refit costs seconds, a schedule is the safer default, with the drift check as a second line of defense. Persistence beat the monthly retrained model in 10 of 50 buildings, so monitoring should score the baseline alongside the model; in the Aalborg district-heating case, with that comparison added, the gated model was retrained in July and beat persistence in six of eight months, where the earlier version had lost to it in five.

In the gate audit, a promoted model beat its reference over the following three months in 92% of cases, whereas refusals were often too cautious; a gate that reads one test window cannot be better than that window. Where false refusals cost more than false promotions, the threshold can be lowered or the backtest added to the evidence. The value of the gates when a fault would otherwise be trained into production was not tested.

## What the data checks caught and missed

On BDG2, which its authors had already cleaned, the checks found that the meters of a whole site held daily totals, which makes an hourly forecast meaningless. They missed months of zero readings at another site, and on ROBOD they missed dead chilled-water meters that independent agent instances found. Both misses have one cause: zero is a valid reading for a meter whose equipment is idle, so a single-column check cannot tell a dead meter from an idle one. Zero readings also mattered in the ASHRAE Great Energy Predictor III competition on the same dataset [@miller2020ashrae]. The needed checks use what the declarations already say: a whole-building meter should not idle for a week, and a chilled-water meter should not read zero while its AHU runs with the valve open. Declaring the scope of a meter and checking related points against each other are the natural next guardrails.

The checks directed judgment rather than replacing it: a CO~2~ sensor floor, change-of-value logging and gaps in camera counts each raised a warning that the agent settled with evidence now in the record. Conversely, the right response to the daily-total meters, forecasting daily rather than hourly consumption, is a judgment that the single configuration used for all 200 buildings did not make.

## Design rationale and hypotheses

Three reasons for the design were not measured and are stated as hypotheses. Rigorous defaults should matter most for less careful users, because they apply whoever writes the surrounding code; in the formative usability study, capable agents told to be rigorous built models of similar accuracy with or without the framework. Comparable records should reduce the cost of review: the eight agents of that study produced eight evaluation designs. And an agent-facing design should lower the cost of applying the same checks to many buildings. Testing the first needs a multi-run benchmark with primed and unprimed prompts, agents of several providers and an auditor blind to the arm.

## Threats to validity {#sec:validity}

Internal validity. The same agent built the framework and ran the case studies, and its transcripts were not archived. Detectors were revised on ROBOD and Aalborg data, so the checks and fault-detection rates are partly in-sample there; BDG2 is not strictly held out, because 20 of its buildings were analyzed earlier. The unit fix in the fault experiment was coded by the author, who knew what had been planted, and was given to every script arm. The TOWT reference is the framework's own implementation with the CalTRACK knots, without the separate occupied and unoccupied temperature terms of the original and not the OpenDSM implementation; a full implementation might be more accurate, which would strengthen the finding that the machine-learning models did not beat it in M&V.

Construct validity. Skill is defined on RMSE, and the promotion threshold depends on that choice. Fault detection is counted per column and type, not per located sample. The M&V test assumes that no efficiency measure was installed in 2017, which the dataset does not document, and the Guideline 14 limits are used as a familiar threshold, not as a claim of compliance.

Statistical conclusion validity. Each task of E1 was scored on one test window, which covered only 3–8 weekdays for ROBOD; the DM tests, bootstrap intervals and backtest address this only in part. The lifecycle experiment covers one year in 50 buildings from one start month, and the Aalborg case one trajectory for each of two tasks.

External validity. The datasets are public and partly curated, whereas commercial BAS exports are messier; even so, the BDG2 sample contained two site-wide meter faults. The room-level records are short, and no weather forecasts were available. The models and searches were deliberately ordinary; stronger models, including deep and foundation models [@emami2023], might beat the baselines more often.
