# Introduction {#sec:intro}

The operation of buildings accounts for about 30% of global final energy consumption [@iea2023], and data-driven prediction underpins many of the ways in which that consumption is managed. Model predictive control needs forecasts of loads and zone conditions [@drgona2020all], demand response needs forecasts of electric power, fault detection compares measurements with expected values, and measurement and verification (M&V) compares metered consumption with a baseline model of what the building would have used [@granderson2016accuracy]. Machine-learning models for these tasks are well studied [@amasyali2018] but rarely applied in practice [@zhang2024b], because each building needs its own model and building one takes skilled effort on data that differ from building to building: point names must be decoded, units are mixed, and exports contain text, placeholder values, stuck sensors and gaps.

Large language model (LLM) agents can now write and execute this kind of analysis code. A preliminary study by Khadka and Zhang [@khadka2024], on which this paper builds, used a prompt template that followed the steps of a machine-learning operations (MLOps) lifecycle and let GPT-4 produce runnable modeling code for a simulated small office in 34 of 35 attempts. Producing code is no longer the difficult step; knowing whether the resulting model can be relied on in operation is: whether it was evaluated without look-ahead, whether it beats the simple rule an operator would otherwise use, whether its error estimate holds in the following months, and what happens to it as the building changes. The preliminary study did not address these questions: its pipelines split time series at random, selected features on the full dataset, used one simulated building and stopped before deployment.

This paper presents and evaluates bemlops, an open framework designed to answer these questions in the same way for every model, whoever writes the surrounding code. An agent (or an analyst) decodes the export and declares each column as a physical quantity with a unit; a domain package then applies the same checks, or guardrails, to every model: physical plausibility, causal cleaning, inputs that cannot reach past the forecast time, time-ordered evaluation against the strongest baseline that uses no machine learning, promotion gates, monitoring and retraining. The evaluation is organized around five research questions (RQs) on the uses that matter for building energy operation:

- RQ1: Across 200 buildings and 72 tasks in 11 rooms and their building systems, how often does a routinely configured model beat the strongest baseline without machine learning, including the time-of-week-and-temperature (TOWT) regression used in M&V practice [@mathieu2011quantifying]?
- RQ2: As an M&V baseline, how large is the annual savings error of the framework's models compared with TOWT, and how well does the error estimated from the baseline year predict the reporting year?
- RQ3: How much does the evaluation protocol, a random split as in the preliminary study, change the estimated error and the resulting decisions?
- RQ4: Over a year of operation, how do scheduled, drift-triggered and gated retraining compare, and are the gates' decisions borne out by later performance?
- RQ5: Which data faults do the checks detect, and what does their handling gain over a quick or a competent hand-written script?

The contributions are an open framework that separates building-specific judgment from checks applied identically to every model, with physical-quantity declarations as the interface, fault semantics of building automation systems (BAS), baselines that include the M&V standard, promotion gates, monitoring and retraining; a pre-specified evaluation on public data, led by 200 buildings of the Building Data Genome Project 2 (BDG2); and lessons for practice on when machine learning is worth deploying, how the evaluation protocol changes energy decisions and how retraining should be scheduled. A formative usability study with fresh agent instances is reported in the Supplementary Material; it is too small to answer any research question. Claims that the experiments do not measure, such as the benefit of rigorous defaults for less careful users, are stated in @sec:discussion as hypotheses. @sec:related reviews related work, @sec:framework describes the framework, @sec:design the data and experiments, @sec:results the results and @sec:discussion their implications and limits.
