# Related work {#sec:related}

## Data-driven prediction in building operation

Machine-learning models of building energy consumption are well established [@amasyali2018] and are a standard ingredient of data-driven control [@maddalena2020; @drgona2020all]. The indoor environment and occupancy are predicted with the same tools, for example occupancy from light, temperature, humidity and CO~2~ [@candanedo2016] and indoor CO~2~ up to 15 min ahead from a year of office data [@kallio2021]. Reviews of energy forecasting call for reproducible evaluation against simple benchmarks [@hong2020energy], and large benchmarks exist for load forecasting [@emami2023; @miller2020]. In the ASHRAE Great Energy Predictor III competition on the BDG2 data, the handling of zero and anomalous meter readings was among the factors that separated the best entries from the rest [@miller2020ashrae]. What remains expensive is the work around each model: understanding, cleaning, evaluating and maintaining it. Much of that work concerns faulty sensors and data problems rather than modeling [@fan2021], and fault detection in building systems is a field of its own [@kim2018; @granderson2023labeled].

## Measurement and verification baselines

In M&V, a baseline model fitted on a pre-retrofit period predicts what a building would have used in a reporting period, and the savings are the difference between that prediction and the metered use. The time-of-week-and-temperature (TOWT) regression [@mathieu2011quantifying], with one level per hour of the week and piecewise-linear temperature terms for occupied and unoccupied periods, is the standard hourly baseline; it is implemented in open tools such as OpenDSM (CalTRACK methods) [@opendsm2026eemeter] and RMV2.0 [@lbnl2026rmv2]. Granderson et al. [@granderson2016accuracy] assessed the accuracy of automated M&V baselines on hundreds of commercial buildings by fitting them on one period and predicting a following period without efficiency measures; in such a pseudo-savings test, any predicted savings are error. The uncertainty of savings estimated from statistical baseline models has been analyzed by Reddy and Claridge [@reddy2000uncertainty]; ASHRAE Guideline 14 [@ashrae2014] gives CV(RMSE) and NMBE criteria; its hourly limits of 30% and ±10% are calibration criteria for simulation models, used in this paper as a familiar threshold.

## Automated and LLM-generated pipelines for buildings

Automated machine learning (AutoML) has been benchmarked for building load prediction [@zhang2023], and an end-to-end automated forecasting framework covering preprocessing, feature engineering and model selection has been proposed [@zhang2024a]; the latter also retrains on a sliding window. Since 2023, LLMs have been used to generate data-mining code for building energy management [@zhang2024b], to automate the training and deployment of load-prediction models [@zhang2025b], to analyze unstructured building data with multiple agents [@xiao2024], and to coordinate specialist agents with many tools for building operation [@jiang2026]. The preliminary study on which this paper builds used prompt templates structured by MLOps steps to have an LLM write modeling code [@khadka2024]. These studies mostly evaluate whether the generated code runs, how accurate the model is on a held-out split, or whether tools are selected correctly. Agents for machine-learning engineering are evaluated on benchmarks that control the held-out test set [@huang2024; @chan2025; @guo2024]; in a building, nobody holds back a clean test set, and the analyst's own split, cleaning and baseline decide whether a reported accuracy means anything.

## Model lifecycle and evaluation pitfalls

Machine-learning systems accumulate hidden technical debt [@sculley2015]; production readiness can be scored with test rubrics [@breck2017], data validated like code [@breck2019] and models documented with model cards [@mitchell2019]. Kreuzberger et al. [@kreuzberger2023] synthesized MLOps into nine principles, used here as a checklist. Mature general-purpose tools cover parts of this lifecycle (@tbl:tools): data validation with Deequ [@schelter2018], Great Expectations [@gong2026] and pandera [@bantilan2020]; tracking, versioning and registries with MLflow [@zaharia2018] and Data Version Control (DVC) [@kuprieiev2026]; drift monitoring with Evidently [@evidently2026]; and time-series learning with backtesting and naive forecasters with sktime [@loning2019], Darts [@herzen2022] and AutoGluon–TimeSeries [@shchur2023]. For building energy prediction specifically, Li et al. [@li2023large] compared static, retrained and continual-learning models at large scale, Derzsi et al. [@derzsi2025performance] compared performance-based drift detectors across 35 HVAC devices, and Heidrich et al. [@heidrich2022adaptively] addressed concept drift in energy time series with profiles.

On evaluation, out-of-sample tests with a rolling origin are the reference for forecasting accuracy [@tashman2000out], cross-validation for dependent data should be blocked [@roberts2017crossvalidation; @bergmeir2012], although standard k-fold cross-validation is valid for purely autoregressive models with uncorrelated errors [@bergmeir2018], and data leakage, including leakage from random splits and from preprocessing on the full dataset, is a widespread cause of irreproducible machine-learning results [@kapoor2023]. How much random splits mislead on building operational data, and whether this changes energy decisions, is addressed in @sec:res-mv and @sec:res-protocol.

None of these capabilities is technically difficult on its own; the contribution is to combine them behind one small interface and to measure their effect on real buildings. Brick [@balaji2016] standardizes building metadata, and where a Brick model exists it can supply the declarations the framework requires; most real exports, including the three datasets used here, carry no such model, so the declarations are inferred from names, documentation and values.
